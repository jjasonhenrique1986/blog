![Cover of the book Platform Engineering, by Camille Fournier and Ian Nowland](/images/eng-plataforma.jpg)

I recently read the book **Platform Engineering: A Guide for Technical,
Product, and People Leaders**, by Camille Fournier and Ian Nowland, in its
[Brazilian Portuguese
edition](https://www.amazon.com.br/Engenharia-Plataforma-l%C3%ADderes-t%C3%A9cnicos-produtos/dp/8575229419),
and decided to write a review of it.

If you've been working in technology for a few years, you've probably felt this: ever faster teams, ever more complex systems and a constant feeling that we're spending more energy getting the basics to work than creating real value.

That's exactly the scenario where **Platform Engineering** fits.

More than talking about tools, the book proposes a **change of mindset**: treat the internal platform as a product, with clear users (developers), well-defined goals and continuous evolution. The authors connect technology, organization and leadership in a very practical way.

Below, I share an overview of the main lessons.

---

## The beginning: understanding the problem

In the first chapters, the book does something essential: it explains why **platform engineering** exists. Not as a fad, but as a direct response to growing technical complexity.

Independent teams, microservices, cloud, CI/CD and security created a powerful environment — but one that is hard to operate without structure.

Here it becomes clear that **a platform isn't just infrastructure**, nor a synonym for DevOps. It exists to:

- reduce friction  
- standardize what makes sense  
- let teams focus on the business domain  

---

## Platform as a product (not as support)

One of the book's strongest points is its defense of the **platform as an internal product**. That completely changes the way you think about priorities and success.

In the following chapters, the authors stress that a platform is only successful if it:

- solves developers' real problems  
- has a roadmap, priorities and metrics  
- evolves based on constant feedback  

Treating the platform as something "everyone has to use" is a recipe for failure.  
Treating it as a product is the path to **natural adoption**.

---

## The developer at the center

Several chapters revolve around **Developer Experience (DX)**. The message is simple and powerful: if the developer experience is bad, delivery suffers — and so does the company.

The book talks a lot about:

- self-service environments  
- well-done automation  
- clear documentation  
- reducing manual and repetitive tasks  

All of that not as a luxury, but as a **scaling strategy**.

---

## Organization, teams and interaction

Another important part of the book discusses how to structure **platform teams**. There's no single model, but there are clear principles:

- avoid becoming a bottleneck  
- have well-defined boundaries of responsibility  
- keep a healthy relationship with the product teams  

The book also covers how platforms communicate with their consumers through:

- APIs  
- contracts  
- well-defined abstractions  

All of this helps reduce **invisible dependencies** and conflicts between teams.

---

## Stakeholder matrix: alignment before scale

A very practical chapter presents the **stakeholder matrix**. The idea is simple but powerful: identify who is involved with the platform, how interested each of them is and how much influence they have.

This matrix helps answer fundamental questions such as:

- who needs to be involved in platform decisions  
- who needs to be kept constantly informed  
- who can block or accelerate adoption  

Using this tool avoids a common mistake in platform initiatives: **building something technically excellent but politically unviable**. It forces leaders to think about communication, alignment and expectations from the start.

---

## The 5 wins and 2 challenges of platform engineering

The book also presents a very didactic model when it talks about the **5
wins** that a good platform delivers, while
acknowledging **2 big challenges** that can't be avoided. The 5 wins and
2 challenges model isn't a technical checklist. It's a framework for
communication and alignment.

The central idea is simple: platform engineering is only sustainable if it
generates visible value for multiple audiences, while also
acknowledging unavoidable tensions. Below is an example of this framework:

### The 5 wins

- faster delivery  
- better developer experience  
- more consistency and standardization  
- built-in security and governance  
- lower operational cost and less rework  

These wins help build **clear value narratives** for leadership, product and engineering.

### The 2 challenges

- risk of excessive centralization  
- constant need for alignment with multiple teams  

The book doesn't romanticize the platform: it makes it clear that success depends on conscious decisions so it doesn't become a bottleneck or an ivory tower.

---

## Security and governance without slowing the team down

A very well-balanced point in the book is how it treats **security and governance**. Instead of heavy controls and slow processes, the proposal is to create **guardrails**: rules built into the platform itself.

That way, teams stay autonomous, but within safe limits aligned with:

- compliance  
- good practices  
- organizational standards  

Security stops being a blocker and becomes a **natural part of the delivery flow**.

---

## Rearchitecting instead of a V2

One of the most mature chapters covers the idea of **rearchitecting instead of building a V2 from scratch**. The provocation is direct: V2s usually fail because they try to solve everything at once, ignoring current users and transition costs.

The book's proposal is to:

- evolve the platform incrementally  
- reduce risks and big bangs  
- allow continuous learning  
- respect what already works  

This view fits very well with product principles, evolutionary architecture and platforms as living systems — not closed projects.

---

## Measuring success for real

You can't improve what you don't measure. One of the chapters is dedicated to **platform metrics**, going beyond uptime or infrastructure cost.

The book talks about metrics such as:

- platform adoption  
- developer satisfaction  
- impact on delivery speed  
- fewer incidents and less rework  

These indicators help justify investments and guide strategic decisions.

---

## Scale, challenges and pitfalls

In the final chapters, the book goes into very realistic territory: **what can go wrong**.

Some common pitfalls it mentions:

- excessive centralization  
- platforms that are too generic  
- lack of product vision  
- disconnection from the teams  

The closing looks at the future of platform engineering and reinforces that it **isn't a project with an end**, but a continuous journey of learning and adaptation.

---

## Conclusion

**Platform Engineering** isn't a book about specific tools. It's about how to build solid foundations so teams can deliver better, with less friction and more autonomy.

If you're a technical leader or work with **DevOps, SRE, architecture or product**, this book helps put a name to problems many people already feel day to day — and points to practical, realistic and sustainable ways to solve them.
